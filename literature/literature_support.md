## 3. Theoretical background (key concepts for the introduction and poster)

**a) Why face-to-face interaction matters for first-year students**
- **Tinto (1975):** whether students stay at university depends on *academic integration* and *social integration*, meaning their contact with peers and staff. First year is the critical period. This is the classic justification for why interaction matters.
- **Moore (1989):** three types of interaction in learning: learner–content, learner–instructor and learner–learner. A useful frame for Sub-RQ1, because a chatbot could *replace* the learner–instructor and learner–learner types with learner–AI.
- **Johnson & Johnson (2009):** social interdependence theory. Cooperative learning (students discussing and building ideas together) improves achievement and relationships. It shows what is lost if groups hand brainstorming to AI (Q5).

**b) Why technology might *displace* human contact**
- **Kraut et al. (1998), the "Internet paradox":** a longitudinal study of 169 people. More internet use was followed by less family communication, smaller social circles and more loneliness, even though the internet is a *social* technology. **Displacement hypothesis**: time spent with technology replaces time with people. This is the classic theory behind our main RQ.
  - *For balance:* the same team's 3-year follow-up found that the negative effects faded (Kraut et al. 2002). Use this to show the effect is debated, which is good critical writing.
- **Turkle (2011), *Alone Together*:** an influential argument that people expect more from technology and less from each other. A useful framing quote for the poster title or introduction.

**c) Loneliness**
- **Weiss (1973):** distinguishes *emotional* loneliness (missing a close attachment) from *social* loneliness (missing a wider network). Sawir et al. (2008) and Diehl et al. (2018) both use this distinction.

**d) AI-mediated communication**
- **Hancock, Naaman & Levy (2020):** define "AI-mediated communication": an AI modifies, augments or generates messages for a person (autocorrect, smart replies, ChatGPT drafting an email). This is the concept behind Q7.

## 4. Literature by research question

### Main RQ: Does AI chatbot use reduce intention to interact face-to-face? (Q3, Q4)

**Crawford, Allen, Pani & Cowling (2024)**, *Studies in Higher Education*. **This is our single most relevant source.**
- 387 university students; structural equation modelling.
- AI chatbots used for information *may* be linked to better performance. Once social support, wellbeing, loneliness and sense of belonging are taken into account, the *net effect on achievement is negative*.
- The authors call this **"human substitution"**: a student asks an AI instead of a librarian, professor or advisor.
- Students who felt socially supported by AI had weaker grades than students supported by friends and family.
- **Use for:** the main RQ and the background. The idea of AI *substituting* humans matches our research question almost exactly.

**Hou, Man, Hamilton, Muthusekaran, Johnykutty, Zadeh & MacNeil (2025)**, ITiCSE '25 (ACM conference paper), *"All roads lead to ChatGPT": how generative AI is eroding social interactions and student learning communities*.
- 17 interviews with undergraduate computing students at 7 North American universities.
- Help-seeking is increasingly *mediated by* generative AI. Students forward peers' questions to ChatGPT instead of answering them, which weakens peer interaction. Some reported feeling more isolated and less motivated.
- **Use for:** the main RQ, Q4, Q5 and Q13. It is qualitative evidence for exactly what our survey measures quantitatively.
- **Limitation:** small sample of computing students only. Our survey adds broader, first-year, non-Western data, which is a **research gap**.

**Kraut et al. (1998)** (displacement theory; see section 3).

### Sub-RQ1: Interaction with classmates and teachers for study tasks (Q5–Q8, Q12)

**Help-seeking from teachers (Q6, Q12)**
- **Seo, Tang, Roll, Fels & Yoon (2021)**, *IJETHE*. 12 students and 11 instructors. AI could make learner–instructor interaction more personalised and scalable, but there were concerns about crossing social boundaries, responsibility and surveillance. Instructors hoped AI would take simple, repetitive questions. **Use for:** Q6, as evidence that AI is changing student–teacher contact, even by design.
- **Griffin et al. (2014)**, *College Teaching*. An exploratory study of why students do or don't attend office hours. Perceived convenience and course factors matter, and office hours are often under-used. **Use for:** Q6 background. Students already under-used office hours *before* AI, so an always-available chatbot may compete with them.
- **Ryan & Pintrich (1997)**, *Journal of Educational Psychology*. A classic help-seeking study: students avoid asking for help when asking feels like a *threat to self-esteem*, for example looking incompetent in front of others. **Use for:** the "I feel less embarrassed" option in Q12.
- **Hou, Mettille, Li, Man, Zastudil & MacNeil (2024)**, ACE '24 (ACM). 47 surveyed and 8 interviewed computing students. Students were adopting generative AI quickly, but it had *not yet replaced* traditional help such as TAs and instructors. Students said they didn't want to "burden" peers and still felt judged when asking people. **Use for:** Q6 and Q12. It also gives a counter-point: AI *adds to* human help rather than fully replacing it.

**Group work and peer discussion (Q5)**
- **Hou et al. (2025)** (see above).
- **Johnson & Johnson (2009)** (see section 3): why peer discussion has value.
- **Gerlich (2025)**, *Societies*. 666 participants. Heavier AI use was linked to weaker critical thinking, explained by **cognitive offloading** (letting the tool do the thinking). Younger participants relied on AI more. **Use for:** Q5. If groups offload brainstorming to AI, they lose discussion *and* thinking.
- **Zhai, Wibowo & Li (2024)**, *Smart Learning Environments*. A systematic review of 14 studies. Over-reliance on AI dialogue systems can harm decision-making and critical thinking, partly because students prefer fast, "optimal" answers. **Use for:** Q5 and Q8.

**Effort and convenience (Q8, Q12)**
- **Abbas, Jam & Khan (2024)**, *IJETHE*. Students under heavy workload and time pressure use ChatGPT more. Use was linked to procrastination and memory loss. **Use for:** Q8 and Q12 ("it is faster"). Quick AI answers are attractive when students are busy.
- **Freeman / HEPI (2025):** students' main reasons for using AI are *saving time* and *improving the quality of their work*. **Use for:** Q12 options "faster" and "better answers".

**AI-written messages and confidence (Q7)**
- **Hancock, Naaman & Levy (2020)**, *Journal of Computer-Mediated Communication*. Defines AI-mediated communication (see section 3).
- **Hohenstein et al. (2023)**, *Scientific Reports*. Two randomised experiments with "smart replies". AI suggestions made conversations faster and more positive, and partners felt closer. People *suspected* of using AI replies were judged more negatively. **Use for:** Q7. AI can help communication, but there is a social cost, which makes a good discussion point.
- **Sawir et al. (2008)** (see Sub-RQ2): language barriers make international students more isolated. **Use for:** Q7 and the Q13 option "helps me communicate in another language". Relevant because many XJTLU students study in English as a second language.

### Sub-RQ2: Social interaction and loneliness (Q9–Q11, Q13)

**Background: loneliness is common among university students**
- **Diehl, Jansen, Ishchanova & Hilger-Kolb (2018)**, *IJERPH*. 689 German students: **32.4% moderately lonely and 3.2% severely lonely**. Emotional loneliness was more common than social loneliness, and both were linked to depression and anxiety. The school-to-university transition gave mixed results. **Use for:** a poster statistic and the Sub-RQ2 background.
- **Sawir, Marginson, Deumert, Nyland & Ramia (2008)**, *Journal of Studies in International Education*. 200 interviews with international students in Australia. **About two-thirds had experienced loneliness or isolation, especially in the first months.** They identify *personal*, *social* and *cultural* loneliness. **Use for:** first-year focus, language and an international context.

**AI as a "friend" (Q9)**
- **Skjuve, Følstad, Fostervold & Brandtzaeg (2021)**, *International Journal of Human-Computer Studies*. Interviews with 18 people who had formed friendships with the chatbot Replika. Human–chatbot relationships develop much like human ones (Social Penetration Theory): trust grows as people self-disclose. **Use for:** Q9. It shows that "treating AI like a friend" is a real, studied phenomenon.

**Does AI reduce or increase loneliness? (Q10, Q11)** This is a debate in the literature. Present both sides on the poster.

*Evidence that AI reduces loneliness:*
- **De Freitas, Oğuz-Uğuralp, Uğuralp & Puntoni (2024)**, Harvard Business School / arXiv working paper. Across several studies, AI companions reduced *momentary* loneliness about as much as talking to a person, mainly because users **felt heard**. The effect appears short-term.
- **Maples, Cerit, Vishwanath & Pea (2024)**, *npj Mental Health Research*. 1,006 student Replika users. They were *lonelier* than typical students but still reported high social support, and they used Replika as a friend, a therapist and an "intellectual mirror". 3% said it halted suicidal thoughts (self-reported, not a controlled finding).
- **Wang, Li, Zhang, Yeung & Wu (2025)**, *JMIR mHealth and uHealth*. An RCT with **100 Chinese university students**: a 7-day CBT-based chatbot reduced depression and loneliness. **Use for:** a Chinese student context. Note that this was a *therapy* chatbot, not ChatGPT-style general use.

*Evidence that AI increases loneliness or reduces socialising:*
- **Crawford et al. (2024)** (see the main RQ): AI social support doesn't give a real sense of belonging.
- **Phang et al. (2025)** (OpenAI and MIT Media Lab). Analysis of platform-wide ChatGPT use plus a 4-week RCT. **Higher daily use correlated with higher loneliness, emotional dependence and problematic use, and with lower socialisation.** These are correlations, not proof of cause.
- **Fang et al. (2025)** (MIT Media Lab), the RCT part of the same project (981 participants, 4 weeks). **Be careful:** the first version reported that heavy use was linked to more loneliness and less socialising, but the *revised* version reports no significant effects of the experimental conditions. If you cite it, say which version you used and call it a *preprint*.

**Synthesis point for the poster:** short-term, AI can make people *feel* less lonely (feeling heard). Heavier, longer-term use is *associated* with less real-world socialising. That tension is a good reason to ask first-year students directly, which is our gap.

## 5. Research gap (why our study is worth doing)

- Most studies are on **Western** students (USA, Australia, Germany, UK) or on **specific groups**: computing majors, Replika users.
- Few studies focus on **first-year** students, who are in the most important period for building social networks (Tinto 1975; Sawir et al. 2008).
- Much of the evidence is **qualitative or small-scale** (Hou et al. 2025: 17 interviews; Skjuve et al. 2021: 18 interviews).
- The loneliness evidence is **contradictory** (De Freitas et al. 2024 vs. Phang et al. 2025 and Crawford et al. 2024).
- → Our survey of **first-year students at an English-medium university in China** looks at everyday chatbots (ChatGPT, DeepSeek, Doubao and similar), not companion apps. It covers both **study interaction** and **social life**.

## 6. Ideas for the poster background section

These are a skeleton to write in your own words, not finished text.

1. **Context:** AI use among students is almost universal. 92% of UK undergraduates use AI tools, up from 66% a year earlier (Freeman 2025).
2. **Why interaction matters:** social integration with peers and staff is key to first-year success (Tinto 1975). Loneliness is already common among students: about one-third are moderately lonely (Diehl et al. 2018), and two-thirds of international students report loneliness (Sawir et al. 2008).
3. **The concern:** AI may *substitute* for human contact. Students ask AI instead of staff (Crawford et al. 2024) or peers (Hou et al. 2025), echoing earlier fears about the internet (Kraut et al. 1998).
4. **The debate:** AI companions can reduce short-term loneliness (De Freitas et al. 2024), but heavy use is associated with less socialising (Phang et al. 2025).
5. **Gap → our RQs.**

**Possible poster statistics** (check each against the original paper before using it):
- 92% of UK undergraduates use AI tools (Freeman 2025)
- 387 students: AI-based social support had a net negative link to achievement (Crawford et al. 2024)
- 32.4% of students moderately lonely (Diehl et al. 2018)
- ~2/3 of international students lonely or isolated (Sawir et al. 2008)
- Higher daily ChatGPT use ↔ higher loneliness and lower socialisation (Phang et al. 2025)

## 9. Small notes on the survey, from reading the literature (optional)

- **Main RQ mentions "intention"**, but Q3 asks for a general prediction ("could reduce … in the future") and Q4 asks about past behaviour. The literature measures both behaviour and attitudes. Consider rewording Q3, or adding one item that asks about *your own* intention, e.g. "I would rather ask an AI chatbot than meet classmates to study."
- **Q12 and Q13 options map well onto the literature:** "faster" → Abbas et al. 2024 / Freeman 2025; "less embarrassed" → Ryan & Pintrich 1997; "communicate in another language" → Sawir et al. 2008. You can mention this in the Methodology section as justification for your answer options.
