## Who answered (Q1–Q2, background, not graphed)

- **Subject:** Business 18 (45%), Science/Engineering 12 (30%), Arts/Humanities 2 (5%), Other 8 (20%).
  - The "Other" answers are Finance ×2, Economics and finance, Data Science, Artificial intelligence, AI, Pharmacy and filmmaking.
  - Most of them belong to the main groups. If you regroup them, you get roughly Business 21, Science/Engineering 15, Arts/Humanities 3, and Pharmacy 1. Say in the Method section if you do this.

## Daily users vs. less frequent users

18 respondents use AI every day and 22 use it less often. The differences between the two groups are small:

| Q | Daily users agree | Less frequent agree |
|---|---|---|
| Q4 less in-person study | 61% | 64% |
| Q6 prefer AI to office hours | 72% | 68% |
| Q8 face-to-face needs more effort | 28% | 50% |
| Q10 AI makes me less lonely | 17% | 14% |
| Q11 would talk to more people without AI | 33% | 45% |

With only 40 people, these gaps (apart from perhaps Q8) are too small to support any claim. Report it as *"no clear difference between daily and less frequent users"*.

## What the results mean, by research question

**Main RQ: does AI reduce intention to interact face-to-face?** *Partly, mainly for studying.*
- 63% say AI has already reduced their in-person study time with classmates (Q4).
- Only 45% think AI will reduce face-to-face conversation *in general* (Q3), and the sample is split on whether they would talk to more people without AI (Q11: 40% agree vs. 43% disagree).
- So the reduction appears in **academic** contact more than in **social** contact.

**Sub-RQ1: interaction with classmates and teachers for study tasks.** *This is the clearest effect.*
- 70% prefer asking AI to explain course material rather than going to office hours (Q6), the strongest result in the survey. Only 10% disagree.
- 60% say their group brainstorms with AI instead of discussing (Q5).
- The main reason is **convenience, not shyness**: "available anytime" 43% and "faster" 18% (61% together), "better answers" 25%, but "less embarrassed" only 13% (Q12).
- Students are split on whether face-to-face talk takes more effort than asking AI (Q8: 40% vs. 40%). They choose AI because it is *always there*, not because talking to people is hard.
- A positive side: 63% say AI-checked messages make them more confident when contacting classmates and teachers (Q7).

**Sub-RQ2: social interaction and loneliness.** *AI is used like a friend, but it does not reduce loneliness.*
- 55% feel comfortable treating AI like a friend (Q9).
- Only 15% say talking to AI makes them feel less lonely, and 68% disagree (Q10).
- On social life, the most common answer is "no effect" (35%). However, 30% say AI makes them "rely on AI too much", which is more than the share choosing either positive social effect: "helps me talk to new people" 5%, "another language" 20% (Q13).

## Connections to the literature (for the Discussion section)

- **Supports Crawford et al. (2024):** students substitute AI for staff (Q6 70%), and AI-based "friendship" does not reduce loneliness (Q10 68% disagree), which matches their point that AI does not give a real sense of belonging.
- **Supports Hou et al. (2025):** help-seeking and group discussion move to AI (Q5, Q6).
- **Partly contrasts with De Freitas et al. (2024)**, who found AI companions reduce loneliness. A likely reason: our students use general study chatbots, not companion apps.
- **Matches Freeman / HEPI (2025):** students use AI mainly to save time (Q12). It also echoes the "always available" convenience factor.
- **Dependence vs. preference** (the tutor's question): the data shows mainly **preference** for AI in study tasks (Q4–Q6). There are also signs of **dependence**, since 30% chose "rely on AI too much" in Q13.

## Limitations (for the Discussion or Conclusion)

- Small convenience sample (n = 40), with Business over-represented (45%) and Arts/Humanities under-represented (5%).
- The survey did not check year of study, so some respondents may not be first-year students.
- Answers are self-reported and show associations, not cause and effect.
- Q8 and Q11 split almost evenly, so there is no clear answer on those items.
- Q13 offered "No effect" with no "Other" option, so students could not describe other effects.
